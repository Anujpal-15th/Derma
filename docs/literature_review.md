# Literature Review: Robust, Fair, Calibrated Skin-Lesion Classification

Scope: skin-lesion / skin-disease classification from clinical and dermoscopic images, metadata fusion, skin-tone fairness, calibration and explainability, as background for the DermaMNIST classical-ML prototype (LogReg, RandomForest, HistGradientBoosting, MLP; macro-F1, balanced accuracy, ECE; MLflow; FastAPI).

Verification rule: every number below was read from the local PDF (first pages / abstract / tables, extracted with `pdftotext`) or from the project context file (`compass_artifact_...md`, "CF"). Numbers that appear only in a journal version and not in the arXiv PDF are marked "(CF)".

## 2. Per-paper summaries

**1. MedMNIST v2.** Introduces a standardized "decathlon" of biomedical classification datasets pre-processed to 28x28 (2D) or 28x28x28 (3D), so they can be used without domain knowledge. It covers 12 2D datasets (708,069 images) and 6 3D datasets (9,998 images). DermaMNIST is derived from HAM10000: 10,015 images, 7 classes, with an official 7,007 / 1,003 / 2,005 train/val/test split. The paper benchmarks ResNets and AutoML tools reporting AUC and accuracy; for DermaMNIST the reported baselines sit at roughly AUC 0.90-0.92 and accuracy 0.72-0.77. This is the dataset and baseline reference for our prototype, and notably it reports accuracy/AUC rather than macro-F1, balanced accuracy, or calibration.

**2. HAM10000.** Releases 10,015 dermatoscopic images of common pigmented lesions collected over 20 years at the Medical University of Vienna and a skin-cancer practice in Queensland, Australia. More than 50% of lesions are pathology-confirmed; the rest rely on follow-up, expert consensus, or confocal microscopy. The authors describe semi-automatic cleaning workflows needed to unify heterogeneous acquisition formats. It became the backbone of ISIC 2018 and therefore of DermaMNIST. Its key caveat for us (CF): multiple images per lesion cause patient/lesion-level leakage if splits are made at image level, and the class distribution is heavily skewed toward nevi.

**3. SCIN.** Uses Google Search ads to crowdsource a real-world dermatology dataset: 10,408 images from 5,033 contributions by US internet users over 8 months from March 2023. Images carry dermatologist differential labels plus estimated Fitzpatrick Skin Type (eFST) and Monk Skin Tone (eMST). 89% of contributions were allergic, infectious, or inflammatory conditions and 54% had onset under 7 days, very unlike lesion-centric HAM10000. Contributors skew female (66.72%) and younger, with 32.6% reporting a non-White identity. It shows that benchmark datasets under-represent the conditions people actually photograph, and labels are weighted differentials rather than single classes (CF).

**4. DDI.** Curates the Diverse Dermatology Images dataset: pathologically confirmed images from Stanford clinics (2010-2020) with Fitzpatrick skin types and uncommon diseases; 656 images from 570 patients (CF). State-of-the-art dermatology models drop sharply on DDI: the arXiv abstract reports ROC-AUC dropping 29-40%, while the published Science Advances version reports 27-36% (CF). Dark skin tones and rare diseases are identified as drivers of the drop, with sensitivity gaps such as ModelDerm 0.41 vs 0.12 and DeepDerm 0.69 vs 0.23 (light vs dark, CF). Robust-training methods could not fix the bias without diverse training data. This is the canonical evidence that headline accuracy on in-distribution benchmarks overstates real performance.

**5. Fitzpatrick17k.** Annotates 16,577 clinical images from two dermatology atlases with Fitzpatrick skin type labels and trains a model over 114 conditions. It finds significantly more light- than dark-skin images, and models are most accurate on skin types similar to their training data. It also compares the algorithmic individual typology angle (ITA) against human FST labels. The dataset is widely used for fairness work, but CleanPatrick-style audits estimate substantial label noise and near-duplicates (CF), so it requires de-duplication before use.

**6. DermaCon-IN.** A prospectively collected dataset of 5,450 clinical images from 3,002 patients in South Indian outpatient clinics, annotated by board-certified dermatologists with 245 diagnoses under a hierarchical Rook-based taxonomy (8 main / 19 sub classes). It benchmarks CNNs (ResNet, DenseNet, EfficientNet), transformers (ViT, MaxViT, Swin), and Concept Bottleneck Models, and explores anatomical and concept cues. Best baseline Swin-B reaches about 70.4% accuracy / 69.7% macro-F1 (CF). Fitzpatrick and Monk labels make it a natural South-Asian external test set. It reinforces that real-world, darker-skin, outpatient distributions remain hard.

**7. Gessert et al. (ISIC 2019 winner).** Describes the winning ISIC 2019 approach: an ensemble of multi-resolution EfficientNets with loss balancing for severe class imbalance, external data for the unknown class, and a dense metadata branch (age, site, sex) fused with CNN features. The best ensemble gets 74.2% balanced accuracy under 5-fold cross-validation but 63.6% (task 1, images) and 63.4% (task 2, images+metadata) on the official test set. That roughly 10-point gap between CV and held-out test is a direct, verified example of optimistic internal validation. It also shows that balanced accuracy, not accuracy, is the challenge metric under imbalance.

**8. de Lima & Krohling (PAD-UFES-20, MetaBlock).** Benchmarks recent CNN and transformer backbones plus multimodal fusion methods on the smartphone-based PAD-UFES-20 dataset. It reviews fusion strategies (concatenation, MetaNet, MetaBlock, Mutual Attention Transformer, cross-attention). With MetaBlock fusion, PiT (0.800 +/- 0.006), CoaT (0.780 +/- 0.024), and ViT (0.771 +/- 0.018) achieve state-of-the-art balanced accuracy. It shows transformers are competitive with CNNs on small datasets and that clinical metadata can be fused with transformer features. Context: the original MetaBlock reports 76.5% balanced accuracy, and a 2025 study reports a +10.43% balanced-accuracy gain from metadata on PAD-UFES-20 vs +2.22% on ISIC 2019 (CF), so fusion gains are dataset-dependent.

**9. Guo et al. (Calibration).** Shows that modern deep networks, unlike older ones, are poorly calibrated: their confidences do not reflect the true probability of being correct. Depth, width, weight decay, and Batch Normalization are identified as factors affecting miscalibration. The paper formalizes reliability diagrams and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for evaluation. Among post-hoc methods, single-parameter temperature scaling is surprisingly effective on most datasets. This is the methodological basis for our ECE metric and a cheap post-hoc fix.

**10. Focal Loss.** Identifies extreme foreground/background imbalance as the main reason one-stage detectors lagged two-stage ones. It reshapes cross-entropy to down-weight well-classified examples so training focuses on hard examples. RetinaNet trained with focal loss matches one-stage speed while surpassing the accuracy of the two-stage detectors of the time. Although designed for detection, focal loss is a standard imbalance-handling baseline in skin-lesion classification, alongside class weighting and resampling.

**11. EfficientNet.** Proposes compound scaling that balances network depth, width, and resolution with one coefficient, plus an NAS-designed base network. EfficientNet-B7 reaches 84.3% ImageNet top-1 while being 8.4x smaller and 6.1x faster than the best prior ConvNet. It also transfers well (e.g. 91.7% CIFAR-100, 98.8% Flowers). It is the backbone of the ISIC 2019 winner (#7) and a natural deep-learning upgrade path from our classical baselines.

**12. ViT.** Shows that a pure transformer applied to sequences of image patches can match or beat CNNs when pre-trained at sufficient scale (ImageNet-21k or JFT-300M). It concludes that large-scale training outweighs the inductive bias of convolutions. The best model reaches 88.55% on ImageNet, 90.72% on ImageNet-ReaL, 94.55% on CIFAR-100, and 77.63% on VTAB. For dermatology this means transformers need strong pre-training to help on small datasets, consistent with #8 and #6.

**13. Grad-CAM.** Introduces gradient-weighted class activation mapping: the gradients of a target class flowing into the last convolutional layer produce a coarse localization map of the regions driving a prediction. It works for a wide family of CNNs without retraining or architectural changes. The authors use it to diagnose failure modes, spot dataset bias, and build trust in model decisions. In dermatology it is the default qualitative explainability tool, though it is only a meaningful contribution when evaluated quantitatively (CF).

## 3. Synthesized review

**Datasets drive what can be claimed.** The field's public benchmarks (HAM10000, ISIC, and the downsampled DermaMNIST) are dermoscopic, lesion-centric, and drawn mostly from European/Australian lighter-skinned populations. Newer datasets (SCIN, DDI, Fitzpatrick17k, DermaCon-IN) expose the gap to real use: smartphone and clinical photos, inflammatory and infectious conditions, and darker skin tones. Results do not transfer across these distributions, and label spaces often barely overlap (CF), so "external validation" is only meaningful on shared classes.

**Headline numbers are optimistic.** Gessert et al. lose about 10 points of balanced accuracy going from cross-validation (74.2%) to the official test set (63.6%). DDI shows ROC-AUC drops of 27-40% (depending on version) when strong models meet diverse skin tones and rare diseases. MedMNIST v2 reports accuracy and AUC only. Under heavy class imbalance (nevi dominate HAM10000/DermaMNIST), accuracy rewards majority-class prediction; balanced accuracy and macro-F1 are the honest headline metrics, as ISIC itself recognized.

**Imbalance and fusion help, but modestly and inconsistently.** Loss balancing, focal loss, and resampling are standard. Metadata fusion (concatenation, MetaNet, MetaBlock) gives clear gains on PAD-UFES-20 but small ones on ISIC 2019 (CF), and Gessert's task-2 (with metadata) test score (63.4%) was not higher than task 1 (63.6%). Fusion should be framed as dataset-dependent and tested statistically.

**Architectures matter less than evaluation.** EfficientNet and ViT bring large ImageNet gains, and transformers are competitive on small dermatology sets when pre-trained. But DDI and DermaCon-IN show that even strong backbones degrade under shift, and robust-training tricks did not fix skin-tone bias without diverse data.

**Trustworthiness is under-reported.** Guo et al. show modern networks are overconfident and that temperature scaling is a cheap fix, yet calibration (ECE, reliability diagrams) is rarely reported in the dermatology papers above. Fairness is evaluated mainly in dedicated dataset papers (DDI, Fitzpatrick17k, SCIN, DermaCon-IN) rather than as routine practice. Grad-CAM is widely shown but rarely evaluated quantitatively.

## 4. Gaps this prototype addresses

1. **Accuracy as headline under imbalance.** MedMNIST v2 reports accuracy/AUC for DermaMNIST. The prototype makes **macro-F1 and balanced accuracy** the primary metrics and reports per-class results, so majority-class performance cannot mask minority-class failure.
2. **Missing calibration reporting.** Following Guo et al., the prototype reports **ECE** for every model (and is a natural place to add temperature scaling / reliability diagrams), something none of the dermatology benchmark papers reviewed here treat as standard.
3. **Leakage and optimistic validation.** HAM10000 has multiple images per lesion, and the ISIC 2019 CV-to-test gap shows how internal estimates inflate. The prototype uses DermaMNIST's **fixed official train/val/test split**, does all tuning on validation only, and touches test once. Limitation: DermaMNIST does not expose lesion IDs, so lesion-level grouping cannot be verified on it; this is flagged rather than hidden.
4. **Reproducible experiment tracking.** Published results rarely ship seeds, configs, and per-run metrics. The prototype logs params, metrics, and artifacts to **MLflow**, so every table entry is traceable and re-runnable.
5. **Deployable, cheap baselines.** Most work jumps straight to GPU-heavy ensembles. The prototype establishes **classical CPU baselines** (LogReg, RF, HistGradientBoosting, MLP) as an honest floor and serves the chosen model behind a **FastAPI** endpoint, showing the end-to-end path that a deep model would later slot into.
6. **Robustness and fairness as first-class concerns (partially addressed).** DermaMNIST has no skin-tone labels, so fairness cannot be measured on it. The review identifies DDI, Fitzpatrick17k, SCIN, and DermaCon-IN as the datasets for subgroup and external evaluation in a follow-up; the prototype's metric code (macro-F1, balanced accuracy, ECE) is designed to be reused per subgroup.

Not addressed by this prototype (stated honestly): metadata fusion (DermaMNIST has no metadata), deep backbones (EfficientNet/ViT), and Grad-CAM explainability. These are the planned next steps.
